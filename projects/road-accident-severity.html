Road Accident
Severity

Data enrichment and interpretable machine learning

French accident records say who was involved and where, but not what the sky looked like. We attached historical weather to 110,124 accidents and measured how much it adds to predicting how severe they were.

Three sources, one row per accident

The accident file has no weather in it. Getting some meant scraping one source, then covering its gaps with a second.

  1. AccidentsBAAC files for 2022 and 2023 from data.gouv.fr: characteristics, places, vehicles and users. They are merged to one row per accident, with the number of users and vehicles added as new columns. The result is 110,124 accidents and 33 columns.
  2. ScrapingSelenium on Weather Underground. Each accident’s coordinates became a city through a REST API, then a time-specific page URL. Over 100,000 records came back with little blocking, but many stations don’t report visibility or ground conditions.
  3. Open dataHourly weather files from the French government fill those gaps. They run to over 2 GB compressed and 20 GB unpacked, so they went into PostgreSQL with PostGIS, where lookups by place and hour dropped from minutes to milliseconds.

Where weather values are missing

The incomplete columns are the weather ones. About three accidents in ten have no visibility value.

Share of the 110,124 accidents, computed from the report’s counts.

Cleaning without deleting accidents

Rows stay. Extreme values are clipped and unknowns get their own category.

Missing and invalid codes
The source marks unknowns with -1. Those became NaN. In categorical columns the NaN became an explicit inconnu category, so uncertainty stays visible to the model. Missing speed limits took the median for their road category.
Outliers
Quantile bounds per variable (average age, number of users, number of vehicles, speed limit), compared at the 95th, 97th, 98th and 99th percentiles and checked against domain knowledge. Values beyond a bound were replaced by the median of that tail, not dropped.
Codes to labels
Numeric BAAC codes became readable categories, and close ones were merged. Road surface went from seven values to four: normal, wet, slippery and degraded.
The target
grav_max is the worst injury among the people in an accident: fatal, hospitalized or slight.

Precision and recall, per class

Precision: of the accidents flagged fatal, how many were. Recall: of the fatal accidents, how many were flagged.

A fatal-accident alert is a trade-off

Catching more fatal accidents floods the alerts with false ones. Trusting the alerts means missing more.

Decision trees and random forests handle mixed numeric and categorical columns and can be inspected afterwards. Each got a hyperparameter search, scored once on accuracy and once on macro F1. A simple forest search covers trees, depth and leaf size, and a complex one adds split size and features per split. The weather runs reuse the same searches and seeds, so the only change is the columns.

Who and when matter first. Weather comes after

Three views of the same question: which columns do the models use, and what happens when one is scrambled?

What the forest relies on

Average age of the people involved ranks first in both runs, followed by day, hour and number of users. With weather added, pressure jumps to second, temperature to fourth and wind speed to seventh. Continuous columns like these tend to score high on this measure, so the checks below matter.

Weather modulates the prediction. It doesn’t drive it

The answer to the research question, and what backs it up.

Main drivers
Who is involved (average age, number of users), when it happens (day, hour, month) and the road setting. Without any weather, models already reach macro F1 0.504.
What weather adds
Accuracy rises by 0.012 and macro F1 stays at 0.504. There are small gains on hospitalized and slight injuries, and a more cautious forest on fatal accidents.
Where it shows up
Pressure, temperature and wind speed sit in the forest’s top seven columns, yet a single tree only uses them below its third level. Visibility shows no effect.
An upper bound
CatBoost, used as a ceiling, reaches macro F1 0.5487, about 4.5 points above the forest, and F1 0.2757 on fatal accidents.

What limits these results

The conclusions hold for this data and these models.

Two years, one country
Accidents from 2022 and 2023 only, all in France.
Uneven weather coverage
Many stations don’t record visibility or ground state, so about three accidents in ten have no visibility value. That makes visibility’s null result hard to interpret.
Few fatal accidents
4,614 out of 110,124. Fatal-class precision never exceeds 0.23 in any run.
Importance isn’t cause
Feature importance and shuffling describe what the models use, not what makes a crash deadlier.
