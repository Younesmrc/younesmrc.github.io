Two text-classification tasks that look alike and are not

Movie: when preprocessing helps, and when it hurts

A controlled study: three preprocessing setups, three classic models, two representations, macro F1.

What the data says first

Perfect balance
Positive and negative reviews are 50/50, so there is no need for class weights or resampling.
Zipf holds
Word frequency follows Zipf's law, so the corpus is large enough to be statistically reliable. The curve also tells where to cut stopwords: a threshold between 10 and 50, taken from the data instead of a generic list.
Names as markers
Odds ratios show film titles and director names among the most positive or negative words, next to genuinely negative terms such as "stupidity", "sucks" or "ludicrous". The report warns that a model may learn a director or a film indirectly, because it happens to sit in the positive corpus.

The protocol

A
No preprocessing, a raw CountVectorizer.
B
Lowercasing and stopword removal, in two variants: stopwords extracted from the corpus with Zipf, or the generic scikit-learn list.
C
B plus Porter stemming.

Each setup is evaluated with logistic regression (LR), a linear SVM and Naive Bayes (NB), first as bag of words (BoW), then as TF-IDF.

What each model wants

Zipf beats generic
Stopwords built from the corpus always beat the scikit-learn list. That list is fixed for general English and removes words that carry sentiment in film reviews.
Stemming never helps
In every model and both representations, reducing words to their roots erases nuance. Stemming plus the generic list is the worst combination.
LR and SVM disagree
In BoW, LR is best with no preprocessing (0.857) thanks to its regularisation, while SVM needs the Zipf cleaning (0.858 against 0.840). In TF-IDF the leadership flips: LR now needs Zipf stopwords (0.852 against 0.827 raw).
NB barely moves
Scores stay between 0.805 and 0.820 in BoW and around 0.80 in TF-IDF. Independence between features makes it robust to the vocabulary, and unable to improve with better preprocessing.

One oddity, NB with stemming and generic stopwords (0.812) beating the same without stemming (0.805), is too small to read as a trend.

BoW beats TF-IDF here

Counter-intuitive, and explained by the task: in sentiment analysis the most frequent words ("good", "bad", "great", "terrible") are often the most useful, and IDF down-weights them.

Tuning on a small validation set

Three configurations, one per representation and model, sent to the challenge to see what really generalises.

Search, then second-guess it

The regularisation strength C was searched on an internal stratified validation set of 200 samples (20 % of training). The report doubts such a set can say anything about 25,000 test reviews and adjusts the values by reasoning: more freedom for LR (C ×10), a much stronger penalty for SVM on a very wide feature space (C from 10 down to 0.001).

What the challenge said

LR + TF-IDF, C = 10
The winner at 82.14, exactly what the reasoning predicted. It was the worst of the three on validation (85.23 against 86.25 and 86.0) and the best on the challenge, so the ranking inverted.
SVM wants a strong penalty
C = 0.001 gives 79.84 against 74.62 for C = 10, a five-point gap.
Raw bigrams plateau
LR with BoW and bigrams stays around 76 whatever the regularisation: they do not generalise on 25k reviews.

Bigrams only, no unigrams

The best model (LR, TF-IDF, Zipf) retrained on word pairs alone still reaches 79.2 F1, three points under the best, with 84.2 precision and 74.8 recall, 80.4 accuracy. Sentiment is encoded redundantly: neighbouring contexts largely rebuild the words that were removed.

What the text reveals

Style
Mitterrand is more argumentative ("on peut", "bien entendu"). Chirac is more ceremonial ("union européenne", "mesdames messieurs"), with collective formulas ("mes chers amis", "mes chers compatriotes") and geographic ones ("États-Unis d'Amérique" and "Saint-Pierre-et-Miquelon" show up among the trigrams).
Epoch
Odds ratios capture time more than style: globalisation, euro and G8 on Chirac's side, Cold War vocabulary, GATT and Maastricht on Mitterrand's. The report calls this a temporal bias, a risk the model learns the decade rather than the speaker.
Stopwords matter
Word clouds are dominated by "de", "la", "et". They hide the differences, and would swamp any vectorised representation.

Baselines collapse

TF-IDF with SGD scores 21.2 F1 (5k features) and 16.7 (10k) on the challenge. They learn the 86/14 split and mostly predict Chirac, so the minority class sinks the F1. Because they reproduce the training distribution, the report infers that the test set is inversely imbalanced, Mitterrand being the majority there. The goal of this part shifts: not to study preprocessing, but to learn Transformer fine-tuning and generalise.

CamemBERT, five versions

Each version fixes what the error analysis of the previous one exposed.

  1. V0 · naiveSplit into shuffled blocks of 50 sentences, which leaks neighbouring sentences of the same speech across train and validation. Context from previous sentences only, a manual AdamW loop without scheduler, no handling of imbalance. On the challenge: 10.0 F1 and an AUC of 3.1, below chance, so its probabilities are inverted. It predicts Chirac for 90 % of the test.
  2. V1 · weights and contextA stratified 80/20 split that keeps 86.9/13.1 in both parts, a weighted cross-entropy (0.575 for Chirac, 3.816 for Mitterrand) in a custom Trainer, and bilateral context: each sentence is joined to its two neighbours on each side within the same speech. Hugging Face Trainer with warmup, weight decay 0.01, fp16, best checkpoint on average precision. Validation F1 0.917, but epoch 2 (0.938) beats epoch 3 (0.916), and a single split is a fragile estimate.
  3. V2 · 5-foldStratified 5-fold cross-validation, weights recomputed per fold, the five models bagged by averaging their test probabilities. Folds score F1 0.912 to 0.938 and the mean reaches 0.953 (+3.6 points). On several folds the best epoch is still not the last.
  4. V3 · early stoppingSame as V2 plus an early-stopping callback with patience 3, three evaluations per epoch, and selection on F1 to match the challenge metric. Validation F1 0.970 (+1.7), the most effective single lever of the whole progression.
  5. V4 · more contextWindow 3 (up to 7 sentences per input) and 384 tokens instead of 256, so the extra neighbours are not truncated. Validation F1 0.968, no gain: longer sequences are harder to optimise in 3 epochs and a 5-sentence window already carries the useful style.

Gaussian smoothing, a post-processing that did not generalise

Sentences of the same speech share a speaker, so smoothing neighbouring probabilities should calm isolated decisions. The best sigma was searched on the full validation predictions of each version.

The gap with validation (about 0.97 against about 0.82) is blamed on distribution shift: the model learned on a Chirac-majority corpus and must generalise to a Mitterrand-majority test. What it taught

Two tasks, two sets of lessons, one common thread: validation is only as good as its resemblance to the test.

More is not better
More preprocessing does not mean better scores. Only corpus-derived stopwords brought a real gain; the generic list and stemming hurt.
Know the model
LR and SVM react in opposite ways to preprocessing, bigrams and vocabulary size. The right choice depends on the representation, which is why their internal mechanics are worth knowing before applying them.
A score can lie
On Président, a validation F1 above 0.9 hid a model, V1, scoring 26 on the challenge. Imbalance, leakage between split blocks and an unrepresentative validation distribution all inflate it.
Tuning can overfit
The best model on validation was not the best on the challenge (LR with TF-IDF), and a smoothing sigma tuned on a validation set unlike the test degraded the result.

Left open by the report: only three epochs of fine-tuning, a test distribution inferred indirectly from the baselines' predictions, and a 0.5 decision threshold never revisited.
